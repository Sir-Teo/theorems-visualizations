## Mathematical and product corrections

**7.13, permutation tests.** The theorem specifies conditional uniform label assignment and fixed group sizes. Exchangeability is stronger than equality of means. A sharp null with complete uniform random assignment gives one route to it; arbitrary randomized/block designs do not automatically give the unrestricted assignment set used here. Within a finite exchangeable orbit, counting the fraction of scores at least as large as the observed score gives a super-uniform p-value, including ties.

For fixed B selected before simulation, independent uniform assignments drawn with replacement make the observed and B simulated assignment scores exchangeable. Their rank, with upper-tail ties, proves validity of `(b+1)/(B+1)`. This is conservative; it is not generally the exact exhaustive tail or the distinct exact with-replacement calibration in Phipson–Smyth §6. Every shuffle button now **replaces** the previous batch. The caption states that repeatedly selecting batches, stopping based on results or moving data after inspecting results is exploratory; no optional-stopping guarantee is claimed.

The former absolute 10⁻⁹ tie tolerance was removed. The dedicated helper encodes each finite binary input as a dyadic integer under a common exponent. For groups of sizes nA,nB, ranking the integer score `N*sumA − nA*total` exactly ranks the mean differences; BigInt absolute comparisons include exact ties. Numeric plotted differences are converted afterward, so their last-bit rounding cannot alter membership in the counted tail. All 12,870 assignments are enumerated. Each simulated mask indexes the same exact tail decision and numerical difference. The two histograms integrate to one. The left axis now expands around every observation; the previous fixed [−3,6] view clipped skewed samples and shifted groups.

Welch's unequal-variance normal comparison is explicitly approximate, and its null model is distinguished from exchangeability. It need not agree with a permutation mean-difference test even for normal data. The Monte Carlo readout uses the fixed-batch add-one estimator's conditional standard deviation, `sqrt(B*p*(1−p))/(B+1)`, reported as a two-standard-error scale; it is not labelled an exact confidence interval.

**7.14, BH's paper.** All fifteen numerical p-values match original p. 295. At level .05 BH rejects four and Bonferroni three, as in that paragraph. Its other named method is **Hochberg**, whereas the display computes **Holm** separately; that modern comparison is now identified honestly. The unnecessary 10⁻¹² addition when counting adjusted p-values was removed.

The source theorem and lemma assert an inequality, not equality under independence alone. The revised statement spells out super-uniform true-null p-values mutually independent and independent of the false-null values. The source appendix directly treats uniform true-null values; the displayed leave-one-out argument also proves the stated super-uniform extension: set a true-null p-value to zero, write R_i for the resulting rejection count, and use the identity `i rejected ⇔ p_i ≤ q*R_i/m`, with `R=R_i` on that event. Independence and super-uniformity bound its expected false-fraction contribution by q/m. Sum over true nulls. Constant-one null p-values show why independence alone cannot force equality.

The 3,000-screen independent two-sided-normal simulation is identified as a reconstruction, not the paper's numerical experiment. Its FDR and FWER are checked directly from screen-level false/rejection counts. The readout reports the largest two-standard-error scales across the displayed procedures, using sample variance of FDP for FDR and the Bernoulli variance for FWER. No sampled FDP is asserted to stay below the nominal level.

**8.13, relevant subsets.** The original source supports Buehler's criterion; the former Welch-1939 uniform-example credit was not established and was removed. The uniform calculation is explicit: for range d, the midpoint is uniform over length 1−d; bracketing the true parameter restricts it to length min(d,1−d). The range density is 2(1−d). These give the displayed conditional coverage and overall one-half exactly. The subset d≥1/2 has probability one-quarter and coverage one, satisfying a fixed positive uniform coverage margin relative to the interval rule. No unique general prescription to condition on every ancillary is asserted.

The former 4,000-point numerical quadrature was labelled exact. It is replaced by closed forms. For d≤1/2, `P(D≥d)=(1−d)^2`, high-subset coverage `(.5−d²)/(1−d)²`, and low-subset coverage `d/(2−d)`. For d≥1/2 the high coverage is one and low coverage is `(.5−(1−d)²)/(1−(1−d)²)`. Their probability-weighted average is one-half. Simulation cut indices use `round(100*d)` for the hundredth-spaced control, removing the .29 binary-floor discrepancy. The readout no longer says both conditional coverages are always far from nominal; it reports conditional Monte Carlo uncertainty as well.

**8.15, Neyman's construction.** The historical discussion is limited to what the actual memoir establishes. Identity (25) is the central inversion argument. The general result now concerns confidence **sets**; interval shape requires the particular model/regions. Modern conservative discrete coverage is distinguished from the paper's constant-coverage formulation. No first-use/vocabulary priority is claimed.

The binomial picture formerly rasterized a thousand-point theta grid, padded counts by ±.5, and joined sampled boundaries by interpolated lines. It now directly draws the 21 acceptance tiles from the 21 equal-tailed CP intervals. Tile thickness is visual padding only; the actual discrete acceptance set is determined by interval membership. The true-parameter count range is computed directly from those intervals. There is no theta grid defining the test. The model is cached across draws and observation changes.

Independent quantile checks also found a roughly 3×10⁻⁹ half-width discrepancy in the older shared normal approximation at level .80. The dedicated model now uses the existing core normal-distribution quantile, matching independent Python quantiles within 3×10⁻¹⁴ across all twenty levels; the older shared helper is untouched. Normal draws are no longer rounded to .001 on the plotted scale. The normal parameter is unrestricted; the actual interval endpoints are displayed and drawn without clipping to [0,1]. Both views expand to contain every displayed interval and the observed interval. The readout reports the model's actual coverage probability, not a blanket expectation of 95 hits: CP coverage can exceed nominal, while normal coverage equals nominal for this known-variance model. Repeated-sample counts include their Monte Carlo uncertainty. The true-parameter handle explores [.05,.95], a displayed subset of the normal parameter space, not a restriction on its interval endpoints.
