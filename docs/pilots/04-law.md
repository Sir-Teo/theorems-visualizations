# P04 · A faulty kettle: which facts and rules matter?

**Research brief · 2026-10-22 · D16 / D14 · researched, not authored or accepted.**

A fictional consumer buys a new, non-perishable kettle from a trader in England and Wales. Readers trace a possible short-term rejection through authoritative rules and identify missing facts. This is an educational case with a fixed jurisdiction and legal snapshot, not a tool deciding a reader's own dispute.

## Sources and temporal boundary

The primary sources are the Consumer Rights Act 2015:

- **`cra9`:** [section 9](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/9), quality standard and relevant circumstances, including disclosed faults.
- **`cra19`:** [section 19](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/19), conformity and remedies.
- **`cra20`:** [section 20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/20), rejection and refund provisions.
- **`cra22`:** [section 22(3)–(8)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/22), timing, exceptions and repair/replacement waiting periods.
- **`govuk-refunds`:** [GOV.UK guidance](https://www.gov.uk/accepting-returns-and-giving-refunds), useful for distinguishing fault from change of mind; the statutory text controls the case.

Read on **October 22, 2026**. The section 22 page states changes known in force through **October 21, 2026**, and displays future/outstanding-change notices. Do not label this as a certified October 22 legal consolidation. The legal reviewer must check commencement and outstanding effects before freezing the lesson.

The period does not simply start on the purchase date: section 22(3) ties it to specified transfer, delivery and any required installation/action conditions. Repair waiting periods and perishables need separate treatment. The first case excludes finance, installation, services and digital content; branches outside that scope stop for further research.

## Reader task and exploration

Audience: general readers, with short definitions of consumer, trader, conformity and remedy.

Use an original calendar case with explicit purchase, delivery, defect-discovery and communication dates. Learners select the facts they need, identify a statutory condition, and distinguish the legal rule from the disputed factual claim that the defect existed at the relevant time. Changing delivery while holding purchase fixed should reveal why the date distinction matters.

State: fictional facts, cited provisions, selected remedy question, and evidence sufficiency. Feedback says “rule applies under these stated assumptions,” “fact needs evidence,” or “outside this case.” It never says an expired short-term period removes every possible remedy.

## Acceptance checks and access

1. Independently calculate day 1, day 30 and day 31 for fixed fixtures; verify calendar arithmetic and delivery-delay cases.
2. Separate undisclosed defect, disclosed fault, changed mind and possible misuse. Do not collapse them into one refund rule.
3. If repair is introduced, model the waiting period and statutory minimum correctly or keep the branch expressly outside scope.
4. A qualified reviewer checks all branches, authority hierarchy, scope and date labels; guidance cannot override statute.
5. A transfer case asks which missing fact prevents a conclusion.

Provide typed date entry, an accessible facts table and step list, keyboard controls, non-color decisions, and explicit assumptions. No drag-only calendar or personal-case upload. A text-only route must reach the same reasoning.
