# P03 · Does reciprocity settle a disagreement in preferences?

**Research brief · 2026-10-22 · D21 / D22 · authored at 0.1.0; independently AI-reviewed for local preview, not public release.**

Use two situated passages to examine how a reciprocity principle becomes an argument about action. The learning goal is to expose premises and objections. It is not to prove that traditions are identical, rank religions, or infer historical influence from a resemblance.

## Reader task and exploration

Audience: general readers; introduce premise, conclusion, counterexample, and normative claim.

Begin with an original hypothetical: one neighbor enjoys music while working and another needs quiet. A learner chooses a proposed action, identifies which preference it assumes, and constructs an argument using one passage. A second panel asks whether swapping the actor and recipient changes the result. The learner may add a premise about informed preferences, harm, consent or fair terms, but must label that premise as an addition rather than words from the source.

State: source passage, proposed action, premises, objections, and revisions. An argument map and equivalent ordered list distinguish quoted principle, interpretation, empirical assumption, and normative addition. A logical checker may detect a missing stated premise; it must not present a moral conclusion as a computed theorem.

## Acceptance checks and access

1. Readers distinguish a passage from their own reconstruction of its implications.
2. The example reveals why assuming identical preferences is contestable.
3. At least two defensible reconstructions and an objection are included in the rubric.
4. A source-context reviewer checks that the comparison neither erases differences nor invents influence.
5. The edition-number discrepancy and uninspected original languages remain visible in provenance.

All argument editing works with add/remove/move buttons and a linear reading order. No required dragging, timed response, or color-only distinction. Provide concise definitions and a complete text version of each map. Formative observation tests whether readers can identify an added premise in a new argument, not whether they agree with the author.
