# P02 · One pond, two translations, several defensible readings

**Research brief · 2026-10-22 · D24 / D23 · working prototype; independent acceptance pending.**

Compare two historical English renditions of Bashō's old-pond poem. Readers should explain how a translator's choices change an interpretation while distinguishing a textual observation from a claim about the Japanese original. The scope is one Edo-period Japanese poem and its later English reception.

## Sources and boundaries

- **`aston-pond`:** [Academy of American Poets, W. G. Aston translation](https://poets.org/poem/ancient-pond/embed). The page identifies *A History of Japanese Literature* (1899) and explicitly marks the poem public domain.
- **`hearn-pond`:** [Hearn, *Exotics and Retrospectives*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/42735/42735-h/42735-h.htm), “Frogs,” printed p. 164 in the digitized 1914 edition; original copyright 1898. The selected rendition uses “jumping.” Preserve the actual wording rather than a familiar secondary quotation. [Catalog record](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42735) marks it public domain in the USA.
- **`ndl-pond`:** [National Diet Library reference record 1000333958](https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?id=1000333958&page=ref_view), created May 2, 2023, updated June 23, 2023. Its Japanese question and bibliographic information were read. It is a reference record, not an original textual witness.

The NDL record supports discovery. The prototype now fixes its Japanese display text to the Koto City museum-linked transcription described below, preserving its spelling. It makes no unreviewed segmentation, grammar or pronunciation claim; specialist review is still needed before acceptance. Do not copy the modern Harry Behn translation mentioned in that record. Hearn's surrounding claims about literary history or syllables are not automatically adopted.

## Reader task and exploration

Audience: readers with no Japanese assumed. Context and reading guides explain the selected punctuation and evidence distinctions.

Display the two English versions with translator/edition labels. The current prototype offers number, image order and punctuation lenses, with two possible readings and a boundary for each. Readers annotate a difference and point to the words supporting an interpretation. A Japanese panel supplies the explicitly named modern display witness; original-language analysis remains outside this first draft. Phrase alignment is a possible later extension and must not imply complete semantic equivalence.

Implemented state: selected lens, annotation category and one evidence-note field. Provide an “observation / interpretation / needs linguistic evidence” selection for the note. Do not award a “correct meaning” score, infer the poet's intention, or equate English syllable counts with Japanese sound units.

## Acceptance checks and access

1. Every quoted line matches the selected edition; original and translator attributions are separate.
2. Readers can locate at least one defensible difference and explain its effect without treating the effect as the only possible reading.
3. Claims about the Japanese language are checked independently; a translation-only observation is not promoted to an original-text fact.
4. A transfer task asks readers to assess a new annotation for whether its evidence supports its scope.

Use stacked as well as side-by-side text, keyboard selection, persistent labels, Unicode and correct language tags, sensible reading order, and a non-drag alignment list. Sound is optional and must have textual equivalents; no Japanese pronunciation audio is currently licensed or recorded. Do not rely on color or hovering.
