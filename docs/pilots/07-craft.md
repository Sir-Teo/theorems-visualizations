# P07 · Make, inspect and repair a paper plain weave

**Research brief · 2026-10-22 · D29 / D25 · researched, not authored or accepted.**

The craft pilot combines a symbolic pattern with an optional physical task. Readers should trace alternating over/under crossings, find a local mistake, and explain what the digital representation cannot assess. A correct grid is not proof of hand skill, tension control or textile knowledge.

## Source and cultural boundary

**`textile-techniques`:** the [Hellenic Centre for Research and Conservation of Archaeological Textiles, “Production techniques”](https://artextiles.org/en/node/185), weaving section, describes plain-weave interlacing and how thread properties affect appearance.

This supports a generic construction exercise, not a claim that plain weave originated in one culture or that the exercise represents Greek textile practice as a whole. The page's images have not been cleared for reuse. The pilot does not copy a culturally specific motif, ceremonial object or restricted technique. A later situated textile case needs its own historical and community sources.

## Reader task and exploration

Audience: beginners. Supplies for the optional activity are pre-cut paper strips and a flat surface; offer large, high-contrast strips and a partner-assisted route. No loom or tool proficiency is assumed.

Start with a 6-by-6 grid, labeling warp and weft. In the simplified checkerboard representation, parity determines which strip passes over at each crossing. Readers predict the next crossing, switch between front and back views, find an intentionally repeated over-crossing, and repair the relevant strip sequence. Explain that an error may affect more than one visible crossing; the digital correction must correspond to a possible physical reweaving.

An original illustrated sequence will show an actual paper sample at key steps. Those images do not exist yet: their creation, material labels and inspection are U2 work. Until they are available, the brief is not a demonstrated physical lesson.

State: grid size fixed for the first slice, selected strip/crossing, over/under sequence, view and repair history. A step list and row/column table mirror every action. Do not add arbitrary decorative complexity before the plain-weave task works.

## Acceptance checks and access

1. A practitioner completes the physical sequence from the instructions and checks that grid states match the sample's front/back structure.
2. An independent check covers row and column alternation, parity after view changes, and a repair that can actually be performed.
3. The rubric separates pattern consistency, completion of the sequence, and observable physical handling. It does not infer tactile competence from clicking.
4. Readers explain why a correctly alternating paper grid cannot predict fabric texture, tension or drape.
5. Formative observation records where instructions, spatial representation or motor demands cause difficulty.

Every crossing is addressable with row/column labels and keyboard controls; provide buttons instead of dragging. Distinguish states using words and patterns as well as color. Supply a linear instruction version, close-up descriptions, adjustable display size and a digital-only conceptual route. The latter is equivalent for the structural question, **not** a claim of equivalent physical-skill assessment.
