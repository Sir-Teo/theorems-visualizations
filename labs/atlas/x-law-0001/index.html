A disclosed scratch and a different alleged defect

Editorial paraphrase

The quality test considers the goods and the circumstances. A condition specifically brought to the buyer’s attention before the contract is treated differently from an undisclosed problem. A disclosed scratch does not, by itself, settle an unrelated allegation about an electrical fault.

Editorial context: Original explanation of section 9, especially (2)–(4). The activity stipulates a consumer, a trader and a paid shop sale; it does not determine that status for a real person.

One route is not every remedy

Editorial paraphrase

Section 19 connects nonconformity with different remedies. The six-month provision in subsection (14) names particular routes; it is not a universal shortcut for proving every issue in a short-term rejection. A recording of a malfunction does not by itself establish its cause or its condition at delivery.

UK legislation publishing service

Rejection and a separate refund clock

Editorial paraphrase

Rejection needs a sufficiently clear indication that the goods are rejected and the contract treated as ended. A question about repair does not necessarily communicate that. The refund provision requires action without undue delay and sets a fourteen-day outer period beginning with the day the trader agrees the consumer is entitled to a refund.

Editorial context: Original explanation of section 20(5)–(6), (15). Return arrangements and this refund-agreement clock are distinct from the short-term rejection clock.

UK legislation publishing service

A conditional thirty-day calendar

Editorial paraphrase

In the unextended ordinary example, counting begins on the first day after all relevant ownership or possession, delivery and any required installation or enabling-action conditions have occurred. Purchase day alone is insufficient. The section also addresses earlier exercise, perishable goods and repair waiting periods. Those exceptions cannot be resolved by applying the ordinary thirty-day arithmetic indiscriminately.

Editorial context: Original explanation of section 22(3)–(8). The calculator uses only stipulated conditions-complete dates. Its dates are examples, not legal-effective dates.

What this exploration can establish

  • Consumer and trader are stipulated roles in these examples. Conformity concerns whether the supplied goods meet relevant contract requirements; a remedy is a legal response to an established failure.
  • A fact stated by the exercise is an assumption, not a document you have independently proved. A missing fact must remain missing.
  • Source inspection: 23 October 2026. Displayed editorial cutoffs are 22 October for sections 9/20, 20 October for section 19 and 21 October for section 22, with outstanding/future-change notices. No common certified legal snapshot is claimed.
  • The examples exclude finance, services, digital content, perishables and installation. Repair waiting-period arithmetic is outside this version.
  • The calendar is computed in whole UTC civil days, avoiding daylight-saving hour differences. An elapsed short-term window does not mean every possible remedy has expired.

Try the idea elsewhere

A receipt is dated 1 March, delivery is dated 5 March, and ownership transfer is unstated. Name the missing fact before attempting the ordinary calendar. Then explain why an unrelated disclosed scratch would not settle a different alleged defect.

What a careful explanation includes
  • Separate the date of purchase from all required clock-trigger conditions.
  • Identify the exact proposition each document supports; do not infer delivery condition from later malfunction alone.
  • Keep rejection, refund agreement and any repair waiting period separate.
  • State which route is outside the model without declaring every remedy unavailable.
