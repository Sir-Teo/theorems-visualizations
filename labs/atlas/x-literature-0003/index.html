Seeing, hearing and imagining in The Cabuliwallah

A narrator can describe an event and imagine another mind.

Read nine selected paragraphs from the explicitly attributed 1918 English witness. Distinguish narrated perception, represented testimony, mental attribution and imagined reunion.

Selected English witness only; no Bengali-language or print-facsimile check. Unperformed participant reviews remain optional.

1918 English school-reader witness attributed to Sister Nivedita, with a preface describing revisions. No Bengali comparison or identification of every reviser is claimed.

Rabindranath Tagore; translation attributed to Sister Nivedita in this edition

T1918 uncertainty · The Cabuliwallah; transcribed pp.4-5

Quotation

I cannot tell what were my daughter's feelings at the sight of this man, but she began to call him loudly. "Ah!" I thought, "he will come in, and my seventeenth chapter will never be finished!" At which exact moment the Cabuliwallah turned, and looked up at the child. When she saw this, overcome by terror, she fled to her mother's protection and disappeared. She had a blind belief that inside the bag, which the big man carried, there were perhaps two or three other children like herself. The pedlar meanwhile entered my doorway and greeted me with a smiling face.

Editorial context: The father disclaims access to Mini's feelings, then narrates her belief. Allow a supported reading of this tension; do not force the whole paragraph into a single evidence category.

Rabindranath Tagore; translation attributed to Sister Nivedita in this edition

T1918 imagination · The Cabuliwallah; transcribed p.8

Quotation

These were autumn mornings, the very time of year when kings of old went forth to conquest; and I, never stirring from my little corner in Calcutta, would let my mind wander over the whole world. At the very name of another country, my heart would go out to it, and at the sight of a foreigner in the streets, I would fall to weaving a network of dreams,—the mountains, the glens, and the forests of his distant home, with his cottage in its setting, and the free and independent life of far-away wilds. Perhaps the scenes of travel conjure themselves up before me and pass and repass in my imagination all the more vividly, because I lead such a vegetable existence that a call to travel would fall upon me like a thunder-bolt. In the presence of this Cabuliwallah I was immediately transported to the foot of arid mountain peaks, with narrow little defiles twisting in and out amongst their towering heights. I could see the string of camels bearing the merchandise, and the company of turbanned merchants carrying some their queer old firearms, and some their spears, journeying downward towards the plains. I could see—. But at some such point Mini's mother would intervene, imploring me to "beware of that man."

Editorial context: Explicitly imagined travel scenes do not establish actual places visited or generalized facts about traders.

Rabindranath Tagore; translation attributed to Sister Nivedita in this edition

T1918 incident · The Cabuliwallah; transcribed pp.10-11

Quotation

One morning, a few days before he had made up his mind to go, I was correcting my proof sheets in my study. It was chilly weather. Through the window the rays of the sun touched my feet, and the slight warmth was very welcome. It was almost eight o'clock, and the early pedestrians were returning home with their heads covered. All at once I heard an uproar in the street, and, looking out, saw Rahmun being led away bound between two policemen, and behind them a crowd of curious boys. There were blood-stains on the clothes of the Cabuliwallah, and one of the policemen carried a knife. Hurrying out, I stopped them, and inquired what it all meant. Partly from one, partly from another, I gathered that a certain neighbour had owed the pedlar something for a Rampuri shawl, but had falsely denied having bought it, and that in the course of the quarrel Rahmun had struck him. Now, in the heat of his excitement, the prisoner began calling his enemy all sorts of names, when suddenly in a verandah of my house appeared my little Mini, with her usual exclamation: "O Cabuliwallah! Cabuliwallah!" Rahmun's face lighted up as he turned to her. He had no bag under his arm to-day, so she could not discuss the elephant with him. She at once therefore proceeded to the next question: "Are you going to the father-in-law's house?" Rahmun laughed and said: "Just where I am going, little one!" Then, seeing that the reply did not amuse the child, he held up his fettered hands. "Ah!" he said, "I would have thrashed that old father-in-law, but my hands are bound!"

Editorial context: Separate the narrator's hearing/seeing, gathered accounts of a quarrel, and quoted dialogue within one paragraph. He does not narrate witnessing the stabbing.

T1918 impression · The Cabuliwallah; transcribed p.14

Quotation

Saying this, he put his hand inside his big loose robe, and brought out a small and dirty piece of paper. With great care he unfolded this, and smoothed it out with both hands on my table. It bore the impression of a little hand. Not a photograph. Not a drawing. The impression of an ink-smeared hand laid flat on the paper. This touch of his own little daughter had been always on his heart, as he had come year after year to Calcutta to sell his wares in the streets.

Editorial context: This witness reads hand. The paragraph combines handling an object with narration about its emotional history; a physical mark alone does not verify all that history.

T1918 attributed thought · The Cabuliwallah; transcribed p.15

Quotation

I remembered the day when the Cabuliwallah and my Mini had first met, and I felt sad. When she had gone, Rahmun heaved a deep sigh, and sat down on the floor. The idea had suddenly come to him that his daughter too must have grown in this long time, and that he would have to make friends with her anew. Assuredly he would not find her as he used to know her. And besides, what might not have happened to her in these eight years?

Editorial context: A sigh is narrated with a mental attribution and rhetorical question. Neither gives direct access outside the fiction to another person's thoughts.

T1918 ending · The Cabuliwallah; transcribed p.16

Quotation

Having made this present, I had to curtail some of the festivities. I could not have the electric lights I had intended, nor the military band, and the ladies of the house were despondent at it. Editorial context: The ending gives the father's thought of reunion, without narrating his observation of a reunion in Afghanistan.

What this exploration can establish

  • The father narrates his encounters with Rahmun and his daughter Mini. His location, hearing, testimony and imagination offer different kinds of access within the story.
  • This witness’s notes attribute the translation to Sister Nivedita. Its preface describes revisions; attribution does not identify the origin of every changed word.
  • The selected hand-impression paragraph reads hand. A different earlier ebook witness reads band; its anomaly remains unresolved. This lesson selects a different identified witness rather than silently correcting or merging the texts.
  • The father’s disclaimer about knowing Mini’s feelings and his subsequent description of her belief create an interpretive tension, not a single automatic reliability score.
02

Explore the evidence

Read all three comparison guides

What kind of access does the narrator claim?

The incident paragraph separates hearing a disturbance, seeing an arrest and gathering an account from others.

Which clause supports which kind of narrative access?

  • Identify the arrest as narrated perception and the preceding quarrel as a gathered account.
  • Read the represented dialogue alongside the narrator’s framing rather than treating it as an external transcript.

The narrator does not narrate witnessing the stabbing; a fictional statement is not an authenticated historical deposition.

Disclaimed knowledge and attributed thought

The father first disclaims knowledge of Mini’s feelings, then gives a belief; later a sigh accompanies an account of Rahmun’s realization.

How does the narration move between outward action and another person’s mind?

  • Read the mental attribution as an empathetic interpretation linked to visible action.
  • Question the narrator’s confidence and compare it with his earlier disclaimer.

Do not collapse an entire paragraph into direct observation, or infer a real community’s psychology from this narration.

A gift, a wish and a thought of reunion

The father gives money and expresses a wish; the ending makes the wedding brighter through his thought of reunion.

Which events are narrated at the wedding, and which scene is imagined beyond it?

  • The imagined reunion can supply emotional closure without a witnessed return.
  • The ending can be read as the father interpreting his sacrifice through a hopeful parallel.

No scene here documents Rahmun’s later journey or verifies the daughter’s circumstances.

Try the idea elsewhere

Select a paragraph containing more than one kind of access. Mark one narrated perception, one report or mental attribution, and a conclusion that still needs evidence. Explain why the 1918 witness must retain its own identity.

What a careful explanation includes
  • Use clause-level evidence, not a single label for an entire paragraph.
  • Distinguish represented speech from direct access to another mind.
  • Keep the imagined reunion separate from observed events.
  • Name translation and witness limits without silently changing the text.

There is no automatic score for your written interpretation. Use the guide to inspect your reasoning.
